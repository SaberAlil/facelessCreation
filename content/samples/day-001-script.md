Nine ninety-nine, not ten dollars. That missing cent is one of the oldest tricks in retail, and it works on careful shoppers too.
> visual: price tag close-up | terms: price tag, shopping, retail shelf | overlay: $9.99 vs $10

Here is the mechanism. We read prices from left to right, and the first digit grabs most of our attention. Researchers call this the left-digit effect.
> visual: eye reading numbers | terms: reading, numbers, attention | overlay: The left-digit effect | claims: C1

So nine ninety-nine gets filed as nine-something, while ten dollars feels like a bigger category, even though the gap is a single cent.
> visual: two price labels side by side | terms: comparison, price labels | overlay: 9-something vs 10

Studies of price perception, including work by Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz, found the effect shows up mainly when that leftmost digit changes.
> visual: research papers | terms: research, study, academic | overlay: Only when the first digit changes | claims: C2

Field experiments in mail-order catalogs also reported that some prices ending in nine sold better than slightly lower prices. Results vary by product, though.
> visual: catalog pages | terms: catalog, mail order, shopping | overlay: Nine can beat cheaper | claims: C3

There is a cost. Shoppers link nine endings with discounts, so many premium brands prefer round numbers that signal quality instead.
> visual: luxury store | terms: luxury, boutique, premium | overlay: Round = premium | claims: C4

So next time you see ninety-nine, round up in your head. You will see the real price, and the trick loses its grip.
