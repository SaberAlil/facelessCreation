Ever noticed that the medium coffee costs almost as much as the large? That may not be an accident. The medium might not be there for you to buy at all.
> visual: three coffee cups in different sizes | terms: coffee cups, coffee shop | overlay: Small. Medium. Large.

Picture a menu. Small for three dollars, large for four ninety. Most people take the small. It is cheaper, and the large feels like a big jump.
> visual: cafe menu board | terms: cafe menu, chalkboard menu | overlay: $3.00 vs $4.90

Now add a medium for four fifty. Suddenly, the large is only forty cents more. Next to the medium, it looks like a bargain.
> visual: coffee being poured | terms: barista, pouring coffee | overlay: Only 40 cents more

Researchers call this the decoy effect. An option that is clearly worse than another makes that other option look better, so more people choose it.
> visual: two options side by side | terms: choice, comparison | overlay: The decoy effect | claims: C1

In a well-known classroom test of magazine subscriptions, adding a print-only offer that nobody wanted pushed most students toward the more expensive bundle.
> visual: magazines on a table | terms: magazines, subscription | overlay: Nobody picks the decoy | claims: C2

It does not work every time. Later studies found the effect is weaker in some situations, and many shops simply price by cup size and cost.
> visual: coffee shop counter | terms: cafe counter, coffee shop | overlay: Not a law of nature | claims: C3

So next time you order, ignore the middle option for a second. Compare the two you actually want, and decide which one is worth it.
